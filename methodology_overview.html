The bottom line

We sorted every market the post-merger bank operates in — 77 metro areas and 4 rural counties — by answering three plain questions: Is the market big? Is it growing? What is our position in it? Size comes from official Census population counts. Growth comes from five hard indicators (people, migration, economic output, jobs, income), each compared against every metro area in America — so "High growth" means growing faster than the nation, not just faster than our own footprint. Franchise stage comes from 30 years of FDIC branch and deposit records for both legacy banks, treated as one combined history.

The result: roughly half our deposit base sits in markets where we're an entrenched leader, and the labels flag exactly where we're expanding, where we're new, and the three markets where we're pulling back.

The growth score: five public indicators, one national yardstick

Each market's five growth readings are converted to "how unusual is this vs. every US metro area?", then blended with fixed weights into one score.

What to notice: the yardstick is national, not internal. A "High" here means the market outpaces the typical American metro area — which is why 47 of 81 qualify: the Southeast genuinely is where America is moving. Job growth carries a lighter 10% weight because its benchmark is weaker (explained in the methodology, §5).

How the standardization works

Each indicator is a 5-year compound growth rate, converted to a z-score against all 925 US metropolitan and micropolitan areas (trimmed of extreme outliers before setting the scale). The cutoffs are ±0.33 standard deviations — a deliberately modest line, so "Medium" means genuinely middle-of-the-pack. Rural counties are scored against all 3,144 US counties instead, so a rural market is judged against its true peers.

Read three things with care before quoting a number

The engine flags its own known distortions on each affected row rather than hiding them.

Big deposit books sometimes move between offices on paper.
Corporate book of record Another office / market Share trend → stage label

The clearest case: Synovus moved its multi-billion-dollar corporate book from Columbus GA toward Atlanta between 2020 and 2021 — a booking change, not customers moving. A detector now flags both sides of every large move (8 markets carry the flag), and the affected labels say so inline.

Shares are "share where we operate," not full-metro shares.

Numerator and denominator cover the same counties we bank in — the right operating question, but not comparable to a published whole-MSA share. And legacy-Synovus tenure is a floor: its sister charters merged into one FDIC number in 2010, so "entered 2010" often means "at least 2010, likely older."

Labels near a cutoff are labels, not verdicts.

Nine markets sit within 2 points of the 8% leadership line (Birmingham at 8.3%, Memphis at 7.8%, Atlanta at 6.5%…), and two stage labels sit on a knife edge (Naples, Orlando). Every row prints its raw inputs precisely so a reader can overrule the label by hand.
